\section{Euclidean path integrals and lattice scalar field theory}

\begin{question}
\diff{Easy} What is a Wick rotation, and why does rotating to Euclidean time make the path integral converge? Contrast the real-time weight $e^{iS/\hbar}$ with the Euclidean weight $e^{-S_E/\hbar}$.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
A Wick rotation analytically continues the time variable $t \to -i\tau$ (equivalently rotating the time contour by $90^\circ$ in the complex plane). Under it the oscillatory real-time factor $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ becomes the exponentially damped $e^{-H\tau/\hbar}$. In the path integral the weight changes from the pure phase $e^{iS/\hbar}$, whose rapid oscillations make the integral only conditionally convergent, to the real positive Boltzmann-like weight $e^{-S_E/\hbar}$ with $S_E \ge 0$. The latter is absolutely convergent and can be interpreted as a probability weight, which is what makes Monte Carlo sampling possible.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Intermediate} Write down the discretized Euclidean action for a free scalar field on a hypercubic lattice of spacing $a$, and explain how $a$ acts as an ultraviolet regulator via the Brillouin zone.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
\begin{equation}
S_E = a^4 \sum_n \left[ \frac{1}{2}\sum_\mu \left(\frac{\phi(n+\hat\mu)-\phi(n)}{a}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi(n)^2 \right].
\end{equation}
On a lattice of spacing $a$ the field lives only at discrete sites, so its Fourier modes are periodic and momenta are restricted to the first Brillouin zone, $p_\mu \in (-\pi/a, \pi/a]$. This provides a hard momentum cutoff $\Lambda \sim \pi/a$: no modes with wavelength shorter than $a$ exist. Loop integrals that would diverge in the continuum become finite sums over the Brillouin zone, so $a>0$ regulates the UV divergences of the theory.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Hard} Derive the Euclidean path integral for a single non-relativistic particle starting from the transition amplitude $\langle x_f | e^{-iH(t_f-t_i)/\hbar} | x_i \rangle$. Show explicitly how the Trotter splitting of $H = p^2/2m + V(x)$ and insertion of complete sets of position and momentum eigenstates produces the discretized action, and state the continuum action recovered in the limit.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
Wick rotate $t \to -i\tau$ so that $e^{-iH(t_f-t_i)/\hbar} \to e^{-H(\tau_f-\tau_i)/\hbar}$, and split the interval into $N$ steps of size $\epsilon = (\tau_f-\tau_i)/N$:
\begin{equation}
\langle x_f | e^{-H(\tau_f-\tau_i)/\hbar} | x_i \rangle = \langle x_f | \left(e^{-\epsilon H/\hbar}\right)^N | x_i \rangle.
\end{equation}
Insert complete sets of position eigenstates $\int dx_k\, |x_k\rangle\langle x_k|$ between the factors. For each factor use the Trotter approximation $e^{-\epsilon H/\hbar} \approx e^{-\epsilon V/\hbar}\, e^{-\epsilon p^2/2m\hbar}$ (valid to $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$), and evaluate the kinetic factor by inserting momentum eigenstates and doing the Gaussian $p$-integral:
\begin{equation}
\langle x_{k+1} | e^{-\epsilon p^2/2m\hbar} | x_k \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar\epsilon}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{m}{2\hbar\epsilon}(x_{k+1}-x_k)^2\right].
\end{equation}
Collecting all factors gives
\begin{equation}
\langle x_f | e^{-H(\tau_f-\tau_i)/\hbar} | x_i \rangle = \lim_{N\to\infty} \left(\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar\epsilon}\right)^{N/2}\!\! \int \prod_{k=1}^{N-1} dx_k\; \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\epsilon\left(\frac{m}{2}\Big(\frac{x_{k+1}-x_k}{\epsilon}\Big)^2 + V(x_k)\right)\right].
\end{equation}
The exponent is $-S_E/\hbar$ with the discretized Euclidean action in brackets. Taking $\epsilon \to 0$, the sum becomes an integral and one recovers
\begin{equation}
S_E[x] = \int_{\tau_i}^{\tau_f} d\tau \left[\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}^2 + V(x)\right],
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the Euclidean action with the sign of the potential term flipped relative to the real-time Lagrangian.
\end{solution}

\section{Gauge theories on a lattice}

\begin{question}
\diff{Easy} Define the link variable $U_\mu(n)$ and state its gauge transformation law. Why is a link variable, rather than the field $A_\mu$ directly, the natural object on the lattice?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
The link variable is the parallel transporter along the edge from site $n$ to $n+\hat\mu$,
\begin{equation}
U_\mu(n) = \exp\!\big(i a g A_\mu(n)\big) \in SU(N),
\end{equation}
and it transforms as $U_\mu(n) \to g(n)\, U_\mu(n)\, g(n+\hat\mu)^\dagger$. It is the natural variable because gauge invariance is realized \emph{exactly} on the lattice through group-valued links: products of links along closed loops are gauge invariant, and one never has to expand in the algebra-valued $A_\mu$. This keeps the lattice measure (the Haar measure over $SU(N)$) compact and gauge invariant to all orders in $a$.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Intermediate} Define the plaquette $U_{\mu\nu}(n)$ and write down the Wilson gauge action. Explain why the plaquette is the smallest gauge-invariant object built from links.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
The plaquette is the ordered product of links around an elementary square:
\begin{equation}
U_{\mu\nu}(n) = U_\mu(n)\, U_\nu(n+\hat\mu)\, U_\mu^\dagger(n+\hat\nu)\, U_\nu^\dagger(n),
\end{equation}
and the Wilson gauge action is
\begin{equation}
S_W = \beta \sum_n \sum_{\mu<\nu}\left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}\, U_{\mu\nu}(n)\right), \qquad \beta = \frac{2N}{g^2}.
\end{equation}
A single link is not gauge invariant (it transforms with $g(n)$ on one end and $g(n+\hat\mu)^\dagger$ on the other). Only a \emph{closed} loop, whose endpoints coincide, has all the intermediate gauge factors cancel, leaving $\mathrm{Tr}$ invariant under $U \to g U g^\dagger$. The plaquette is the shortest such closed loop on a hypercubic lattice, hence the smallest gauge-invariant object.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Hard} By expanding the link variables in powers of the lattice spacing $a$, show that the Wilson gauge action reduces to the continuum Yang--Mills action $\frac{1}{4}\int d^4x\, F_{\mu\nu}^a F^{a\,\mu\nu}$ as $a\to 0$. Identify the leading lattice-artifact correction.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
Write $U_\mu(n) = e^{iagA_\mu(n)}$ and use the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula to combine the four exponentials of the plaquette. The linear terms in $A$ cancel around the closed loop; the surviving terms assemble into the field strength
\begin{equation}
U_{\mu\nu}(n) = \exp\!\Big(i a^2 g F_{\mu\nu}(n) + \mathcal{O}(a^3)\Big), \qquad F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu + ig[A_\mu, A_\nu].
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
1 - \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,U_{\mu\nu} = \frac{a^4 g^2}{2N}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\big) + \mathcal{O}(a^6).
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $\beta = 2N/g^2$ and summing, $a^4\sum_n \to \int d^4x$, gives
\begin{equation}
S_W \to \frac{1}{2}\int d^4x\, \mathrm{Tr}\, F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{4}\int d^4x\, F_{\mu\nu}^a F^{a}_{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
the continuum Yang--Mills action. The leading correction is $\mathcal{O}(a^2)$ (the $a^6$ term relative to the $a^4$ leading term), a dimension-six operator; it is this $\mathcal{O}(a^2)$ artifact that improved actions (e.g.\ Symanzik improvement) are designed to cancel.
\end{solution}

\section{Fermions on a lattice}

\begin{question}
\diff{Easy} State the fermion doubling problem. How many doublers does the naive discretization of the Dirac operator produce in $d$ dimensions, and where in the Brillouin zone do they sit?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
Replacing $\gamma_\mu\partial_\mu$ by the symmetric difference $\gamma_\mu\frac{\psi(n+\hat\mu)-\psi(n-\hat\mu)}{2a}$ gives an inverse propagator $\propto \frac{i}{a}\sum_\mu \gamma_\mu \sin(p_\mu a)$, which vanishes not only at $p_\mu=0$ but at every $p_\mu \in \{0, \pi/a\}$. Each momentum component thus contributes two zeros, giving $2^d$ degenerate species; in $d=4$ this is $16$ fermions where the theory should describe one. The extra $15$ doublers sit at the corners and edge-centers of the Brillouin zone (momentum components equal to $\pi/a$).
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Intermediate} Compare Wilson and staggered fermions. What symmetry does each approach sacrifice or preserve, and by what mechanism does each remove (or reduce) the doublers?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
\emph{Wilson fermions} add a dimension-five term $-\frac{ar}{2}\sum_\mu \Delta_\mu\psi$ (a discretized Laplacian) that gives each doubler a mass $\sim 1/a$, so the $15$ doublers decouple as $a\to 0$ and only the physical mode survives. The price is that this term explicitly breaks chiral symmetry at finite $a$, requiring an additive mass renormalization / fine-tuning to reach the chiral limit. \emph{Staggered (Kogut--Susskind) fermions} instead spread the four Dirac components over the $2^4$ corners of a hypercube (spin diagonalization), reducing the $16$ species to $4$ "tastes." This preserves a remnant $U(1)$ chiral symmetry at finite $a$ (protecting the mass), but the residual four-fold taste degeneracy must be removed (e.g.\ by the fourth-root trick), which introduces taste-breaking lattice artifacts.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Hard} State the Nielsen--Ninomiya no-go theorem and its assumptions. Explain how the Ginsparg--Wilson relation evades it and what form of lattice chiral symmetry it permits.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
The Nielsen--Ninomiya theorem states that a lattice Dirac operator cannot simultaneously be (i) local, (ii) translation invariant, (iii) chirally symmetric in the naive sense $\{D,\gamma_5\}=0$, and (iv) free of doublers (having the correct continuum limit). Any operator satisfying (i), (ii), (iv) must break exact chiral symmetry. The Ginsparg--Wilson relation
\begin{equation}
\{D, \gamma_5\} = a\, D\,\gamma_5\, D
\end{equation}
evades the theorem by relaxing exact anticommutation: the right-hand side is an $\mathcal{O}(a)$ term that vanishes in the continuum. A $D$ satisfying it (e.g.\ the overlap operator) is local and doubler-free, and is invariant under the L\"uscher lattice chiral transformation $\delta\psi = \gamma_5(1-\tfrac{a}{2}D)\psi$, $\delta\bar\psi = \bar\psi(1-\tfrac{a}{2}D)\gamma_5$. This is an exact, modified chiral symmetry on the lattice that reproduces the correct chiral anomaly and index theorem, circumventing the no-go obstruction without reintroducing doublers.
\end{solution}

\section{Continuum limit}

\begin{question}
\diff{Easy} What is the physical correlation length $\xi_{\rm phys}$ in terms of the lattice spacing $a$ and the dimensionless correlation length $\xi$? Why must $\xi \to \infty$ (in lattice units) to reach the continuum?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
The physical correlation length is $\xi_{\rm phys} = a\,\xi$, where $\xi$ is measured in lattice units (the number of lattice sites over which correlations decay). Equivalently $\xi = 1/(a m_{\rm phys})$ for a physical mass $m_{\rm phys}$. To take $a\to 0$ while keeping $\xi_{\rm phys}$ (a physical quantity) fixed, the dimensionless $\xi = \xi_{\rm phys}/a$ must diverge. A diverging correlation length in lattice units is precisely the hallmark of a critical point, so the continuum limit lives at a critical point of the lattice model.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Intermediate} Explain why the continuum limit must be taken at a second-order (critical) point of the lattice theory, and why a first-order transition is unsuitable.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
At a second-order phase transition the correlation length diverges \emph{continuously} and there is no latent heat, so one can tune the bare couplings to make $\xi \to \infty$ smoothly; this is what lets $a = \xi_{\rm phys}/\xi \to 0$ while physics stays fixed, and it also guarantees loss of memory of the lattice (universality). At a first-order transition the correlation length stays \emph{finite} across the transition (two phases coexist with a discontinuity and latent heat), so $\xi$ never diverges and $a$ cannot be sent to zero — there is no scaling region in which lattice artifacts vanish. Hence only second-order critical points define a continuum limit.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Hard} Using asymptotic freedom and the two-loop beta function of $SU(N)$ gauge theory, derive how the lattice spacing $a$ depends on the bare coupling $g$ near the continuum limit. Explain what "asymptotic scaling" means and how it is used to set the lattice scale.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
The bare coupling runs with the cutoff $\Lambda = 1/a$ according to
\begin{equation}
-a\frac{dg}{da} = \beta(g) = -\beta_0 g^3 - \beta_1 g^5 - \cdots, \qquad
\beta_0 = \frac{1}{(4\pi)^2}\frac{11N}{3}, \quad \beta_1 = \frac{1}{(4\pi)^4}\frac{34}{3}N^2 .
\end{equation}
Integrating the two-loop equation gives the lattice spacing in terms of $g$:
\begin{equation}
a\,\Lambda_L = \big(\beta_0 g^2\big)^{-\beta_1/2\beta_0^2}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\beta_0 g^2}\right)\big[1+\mathcal{O}(g^2)\big],
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda_L$ is the lattice scale (a renormalization-group invariant integration constant). Since $\beta_0>0$, $a\to 0$ as $g\to 0$: the continuum limit is the weak-coupling limit, i.e.\ asymptotic freedom. "Asymptotic scaling" is the regime where this two-loop formula holds, so that every dimensionful lattice quantity behaves as (its value in lattice units) $\times\, a(g)$ with the same exponential dependence on $g$; observing that dimensionless ratios become $g$-independent confirms one is in the scaling window. In practice one measures a known physical quantity (e.g.\ a hadron mass or the string tension) in lattice units at a given $g$ and uses $a(g)$ to convert all other lattice results to physical units.
\end{solution}

\section{Chemical potential and the numerical sign problem}

\begin{question}
\diff{Easy} How is a quark chemical potential $\mu$ introduced into the lattice action, and why can it not simply multiply the number operator naively? State qualitatively why $\mu \neq 0$ leads to a complex action.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
A chemical potential couples to conserved quark number. On the lattice a naive term $\mu\,\bar\psi\gamma_0\psi$ produces $\mu^2/a^2$ divergences in the continuum limit. The cure (Hasenfratz--Karsch) is to introduce $\mu$ like an imaginary constant temporal gauge field, multiplying the forward and backward temporal hopping terms by $e^{+\mu a}$ and $e^{-\mu a}$ respectively. This exponential (link-like) coupling is finite in the continuum. Because the two temporal directions are weighted asymmetrically ($e^{+\mu a} \neq e^{-\mu a}$ for real $\mu$), the Dirac operator loses its $\gamma_5$-Hermiticity and its determinant becomes complex.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Intermediate} Explain the origin of the sign problem in terms of $\gamma_5$-Hermiticity of the Dirac operator. Why does reweighting from a positive ensemble fail as the volume grows?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
At $\mu=0$ the Dirac operator obeys $\gamma_5$-Hermiticity, $M^\dagger = \gamma_5 M \gamma_5$, which forces $\det M$ to be real (and, for degenerate pairs, positive), so it can serve as a probability weight. The finite-density coupling $e^{\pm\mu a}$ breaks this relation: instead $M(\mu)^\dagger = \gamma_5 M(-\mu^*)\gamma_5$, so for real $\mu$ the determinant is genuinely complex. Importance sampling then has no positive weight to sample from. One can reweight, writing observables as ratios of averages over a positive ensemble (e.g.\ phase-quenched), but the reweighting factor is the fluctuating phase $e^{i\theta}$ whose average $\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle \sim e^{-V\Delta f}$ is exponentially small in the volume. The signal-to-noise ratio therefore degrades exponentially with $V$, requiring an exponentially large number of configurations — the sign problem.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Hard} Quantify the severity of the sign problem via the expectation value of the phase $\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{pq}$ in the phase-quenched ensemble, and show that it is suppressed exponentially in the volume, $\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle \sim e^{-V \Delta f}$. Relate $\Delta f$ to a free-energy difference and explain the resulting cost of a reweighted Monte Carlo estimate.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
Split the complex weight as $\det M(\mu) = |\det M(\mu)|\, e^{i\theta}$. The full and phase-quenched partition functions are $Z = \int \mathcal{D}U\, |\det M|\, e^{i\theta}\, e^{-S_g}$ and $Z_{pq} = \int \mathcal{D}U\, |\det M|\, e^{-S_g}$. Their ratio is exactly the average phase in the phase-quenched ensemble:
\begin{equation}
\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{pq} = \frac{Z}{Z_{pq}} = e^{-V(f - f_{pq})} \equiv e^{-V\Delta f},
\end{equation}
where $f$ and $f_{pq}$ are the free-energy densities of the full and phase-quenched theories and $\Delta f = f - f_{pq} \ge 0$. Since $\Delta f$ is generically nonzero (the phase-quenched theory has different physics — e.g.\ a pion condensate — so its free energy differs), the average phase is exponentially small in the volume. Reweighting estimates $\langle O\rangle = \langle O e^{i\theta}\rangle_{pq}/\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{pq}$; the statistical error of the denominator relative to its value grows like $1/(\sqrt{N_{\rm cfg}}\,\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{pq})$. To keep it fixed one needs
\begin{equation}
N_{\rm cfg} \gtrsim \langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{pq}^{-2} \sim e^{2V\Delta f},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ an exponentially large number of configurations in the volume — the quantitative statement of the sign problem's severity.
\end{solution}

\section{Techniques to circumvent the sign problem}

\begin{question}
\diff{Easy} Distinguish sign quenching from phase quenching. Why is the phase-quenched theory generally not the theory one wants to solve?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
Sign quenching replaces the (real but possibly negative) weight by its absolute value, discarding the sign; phase quenching replaces the complex weight $\det M = |\det M| e^{i\theta}$ by its modulus $|\det M|$, discarding the phase. In QCD phase quenching corresponds to giving the anti-quark an opposite chemical potential, i.e.\ simulating an isospin chemical potential rather than a baryon one. That theory has genuinely different physics (e.g.\ a charged pion-condensation transition at $\mu \approx m_\pi/2$) and hence a different free energy, so it does not reproduce the finite-baryon-density results one actually wants — the discarded phase carries the essential physics.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Intermediate} Describe two conventional finite-density approaches (e.g.\ Taylor expansion in $\mu/T$, imaginary chemical potential with analytic continuation, or reweighting). For each, state its domain of validity and its main limitation.
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
\emph{Taylor expansion:} simulate at $\mu=0$ (where the weight is positive) and expand observables in powers of $\mu/T$, computing the Taylor coefficients as $\mu=0$ correlators. Valid for small $\mu/T$; limited by the radius of convergence (set by the nearest singularity, e.g.\ a critical endpoint) and by the rapidly growing statistical noise of high-order coefficients. \emph{Imaginary chemical potential:} for $\mu = i\mu_I$ the determinant is real and positive, so one simulates directly and then analytically continues results back to real $\mu$. Valid within the first Roberge--Weiss sector $\mu_I/T < \pi/3$; limited by the ansatz used for analytic continuation and by the RW transition bounding the accessible region. (\emph{Reweighting}, a third option, is exact in principle but suffers the exponential overlap/sign degradation discussed above.) All three are controlled only at small chemical potential and cannot reach the cold, dense region of the phase diagram.
\end{solution}

\begin{question}
\diff{Hard} Explain the idea of Lefschetz thimbles: how deforming the integration contour into complexified field space onto steepest-descent manifolds tames the sign problem. What new difficulties (e.g.\ residual phase, multiple thimbles, Jacobian) does the method introduce?
\end{question}
\begin{solution}
The integrand $e^{-S}$ with complex action $S$ is holomorphic, so by Cauchy's theorem the integration contour over real fields can be deformed into complexified field space without changing the integral. One deforms it onto a union of \emph{Lefschetz thimbles} — the steepest-descent (unstable) manifolds attached to the critical points $\partial S=0$, defined by the holomorphic gradient flow $\dot z = \overline{\partial S/\partial z}$. On a thimble the imaginary part $\mathrm{Im}\,S$ is \emph{constant} (equal to its value at the critical point), so the rapidly oscillating phase that caused the sign problem is factored out and $e^{-\mathrm{Re}\,S}$ provides a real, positive sampling weight. The new difficulties are: (i) a \emph{residual phase} from the orientation of the thimble — the Jacobian of the tangent-space frame (the induced measure) is itself complex and must be reweighted, though its fluctuations are milder; (ii) \emph{multiple thimbles} — several critical points may contribute, weighted by integer intersection numbers, and combining them coherently can reintroduce cancellations (a "global" sign problem); (iii) the practical cost of \emph{integrating the gradient flow} and computing the Jacobian ($\det$ of the flow) at every step, which is numerically expensive; and (iv) subtleties near \emph{Stokes phenomena}, where the set of contributing thimbles changes discontinuously as parameters vary.
\end{solution}
